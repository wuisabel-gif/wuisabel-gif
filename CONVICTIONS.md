# Convictions

## The same structures appear everywhere.

Modern academia is organized into disciplines. Computer science, biology, economics, psychology, robotics, and dozens of other fields each possess their own vocabulary, journals, and traditions.

Yet the deeper I study, the less fundamental those boundaries appear.

A search problem remains a search problem whether the goal is routing an underwater robot or selecting piano fingerings. Probabilistic inference appears in robotics, genetics, finance, and neuroscience. Strategic interactions emerge in international relations, markets, organizations, and friendships.

The domain changes. The structure often does not.

Many of the most powerful ideas are not confined to a single field. They migrate. Information theory moved from telecommunications into linguistics and machine learning. Evolutionary principles appear in biology, optimization, and economics. Graphs describe social networks, transportation systems, and neural architectures alike.

Academic disciplines are useful organizational tools, but reality itself does not recognize departmental boundaries.

Many of my projects begin when I encounter a familiar structure in an unfamiliar place. What appears to be a new problem is often an old pattern wearing different terminology.

Progress frequently comes not from discovering entirely new ideas, but from recognizing that an idea developed in one domain can be applied in another.

Different problems often conceal the same form.

## Technology requires participation

There is a common tendency, especially among engineers, to think of technology primarily in terms of invention.

A new algorithm is discovered. A new machine is built. A new system is deployed.

And these achievements are indeed important.

Yet history suggests that invention alone is rarely what changes the world.

Electricity is an obvious example. One could imagine a civilization discovering electrical phenomena and recording them in textbooks. Such a discovery would be intellectually interesting. But it would not transform society.

What transformed society was something else. Millions of ordinary people incorporated electricity into daily life. Homes were lit. Factories were reorganized. Cities changed their rhythms. New habits emerged. New possibilities became normal.

The invention mattered because people participated in it.

The same principle appears repeatedly.

Technology is often described as replacing human effort. Sometimes it does.

But many of the most successful technologies achieve something different. They create new ways for people to engage with the world and with one another.

The internet became important because millions of people wrote websites, uploaded photographs, answered questions, and shared ideas. GPS became important because people used it to navigate unfamiliar places. Social networks became important because people filled them with relationships.

Without participation, even remarkable technologies remain curiosities.

The most valuable tools are rarely those that remove people from the process entirely. They are the ones that allow ordinary people to contribute, explore, create, and discover things that would otherwise have been difficult or impossible.

Technology does not change the world by existing.

It changes the world when people make it part of their lives.

## Understanding is becoming more important than execution.

For much of modern technological history, execution was scarce.

Ideas were plentiful. Implementation was expensive.

Today that balance is changing.

Artificial intelligence has dramatically lowered the cost of producing software, documents, images, and analysis. Many tasks that once required substantial technical labor can now be completed in minutes.

This development has led some to conclude that technical skill is becoming irrelevant.

The opposite conclusion seems closer to the truth.

When execution becomes cheaper, the quality of the objective becomes more important. The central challenge is no longer merely building things. It is determining what ought to be built.

A vague objective pursued efficiently remains a vague objective.

A well-defined problem remains valuable even when implementation becomes easy.

The ability to communicate clearly, identify hidden assumptions, clarify goals, and formulate questions grows more important as tools become more powerful.

The bottleneck is moving from production to understanding.

And understanding has always been difficult.

## Do not underestimate brute force.

There is a recurring temptation in engineering to search for elegant solutions before fully understanding the problem.

A difficult problem seems to demand a sophisticated algorithm. A growing codebase seems to demand another abstraction. A slow program seems to demand optimization.

Sometimes these instincts are correct.

Often they are premature.

Brute force has an undeserved reputation. It is frequently dismissed as inelegant, unsophisticated, or temporary. Yet many successful systems rely on some form of brute force simply because modern computers are remarkably fast and many real-world problems are smaller than they first appear.

A brute-force search can outperform a clever heuristic when the search space is manageable. A linear scan can outperform a complex index when the dataset is small. A spreadsheet can outperform a database when there are only a few dozen rows. A USB drive can outperform a distributed system when all that is required is moving a file.

The goal of engineering is not to demonstrate cleverness.

The goal is to solve problems.

Complexity carries costs. It must be understood, maintained, debugged, documented, and eventually replaced. Every layer of sophistication introduces new assumptions and new opportunities for failure.

For that reason, I prefer to begin with the simplest approach that could reasonably work.

If brute force is sufficient, there is no shame in using it.

If reality demands something more sophisticated, reality will make that clear soon enough.

Many engineering mistakes begin not with too little complexity, but with too much of it.
