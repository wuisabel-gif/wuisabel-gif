# Freedom, Artificial Intelligence, and the Fall

Last semester, while taking British Literature, I spent several weeks reading Paradise Lost. Like many readers, I had known the broad outline of the story since childhood. There was the serpent, the forbidden fruit, humanity's first act of disobedience, and the expulsion from Eden. Yet reading Milton alongside Genesis made me realize that I had been asking the same questions everyone else asks. Why did the serpent tempt Eve? Why did Adam listen? Why did humanity fall? These are certainly important questions. But there is another question that seems almost too obvious to ask.

Why was the tree there at all?

If God knew what would happen, why create the tree of the knowledge of good and evil? Why not simply omit it from the garden? Without the tree there would have been no forbidden fruit, no temptation, and, it would seem, no Fall. One naturally thinks that if the possibility of failure were removed, failure itself would disappear. It is an attractive thought. It is also, I think, incomplete.

The first thing worth noticing is that Scripture never describes the tree as evil. Indeed, everything about the narrative suggests the opposite. The tree belongs to creation, and creation is repeatedly declared good. Nor is knowledge itself treated as something corrupting. Proverbs exhorts us to seek wisdom. Solomon asks for understanding rather than wealth. Throughout Scripture, knowledge rightly ordered is presented as one of God's gifts. Whatever the tree represents, it cannot simply be knowledge, because knowledge itself is repeatedly commended.

Nor does the serpent seem to provide the deepest explanation. The serpent tempts, certainly, but temptation is not compulsion. It introduces no new possibility into the world. It merely directs attention toward one that already exists. Had the tree not existed, the serpent would have had nothing to point toward. Had human beings possessed no freedom, the serpent's words would have been meaningless. The serpent explains why the choice became immediate. It does not explain why the choice was possible.

The question therefore shifts. Perhaps the deepest issue is neither the serpent nor the tree. Perhaps it is freedom itself.

Suppose God wished to create creatures capable of genuine love, trust, obedience, gratitude, and friendship. Such goods appear inseparable from freedom. Love that cannot be refused scarcely deserves the name. Obedience that is impossible to reject resembles programming more than faithfulness. A world populated by creatures incapable of choosing otherwise might be orderly, but it is difficult to see how it could contain the highest forms of relationship. Freedom, if it is genuine, necessarily includes the possibility of its misuse.

If this is correct, then the tree begins to appear in a different light. It is not evidence that God desired the Fall. It is evidence that God desired creatures whose obedience could be meaningful because it was freely given. Remove every possibility of rejecting the good, and one has not merely removed evil. One has also removed the possibility of freely choosing the good. The tree, then, is not the cause of evil. It is the condition under which freedom becomes real.

This distinction has caused me to think differently about artificial intelligence.

Much of the current discussion begins with a surprisingly simple assumption. Artificial intelligence, we are told, is either merely a tool or it is becoming a replacement for humanity. It is either a sophisticated calculator or an emerging superintelligence. The debate often proceeds as though these were the only two categories available.

I have become increasingly doubtful that they are.

Calling AI "just a tool" seems less convincing with every passing year. A hammer has no objectives. A telescope does not revise its plans when new information arrives. A calculator does not decide that another approach would be more efficient. Modern AI agents increasingly decompose objectives into subtasks, invoke tools, monitor progress, revise plans when they fail, and decide what information they require next. They exhibit forms of practical autonomy that earlier software simply did not possess.

Yet it also seems premature to describe them as independent moral persons.

Here I think an important distinction is often overlooked.

Agency is not identical with personhood.

Nor is agency identical with moral responsibility.

Human civilization has always recognized different kinds of agency. A corporation signs contracts, owns property, and can be sued in court, yet no one supposes that a corporation possesses consciousness. Governments negotiate treaties and make decisions extending over generations, yet governments are not biological organisms. Domesticated animals exhibit learning, preference, and initiative. We do not therefore regard them as moral equals. There already exists a spectrum between inert objects and fully responsible persons.

Artificial intelligence seems to be moving somewhere within that spectrum.

This observation also makes me question another common description.

People often say that AI should remain our servant.

That language initially sounds reassuring. It emphasizes human authority rather than machine authority. Yet the analogy deserves closer examination.

A hammer is a tool.

A servant is not.

A servant possesses initiative. A servant decides how best to accomplish a task. A servant notices mistakes, asks questions, adapts to changing circumstances, and occasionally even disagrees with instructions when they appear mistaken. Indeed, many of the qualities we admire in an excellent servant—judgment, foresight, reliability, adaptability—are themselves forms of agency. The very analogy to servanthood quietly acknowledges that AI is becoming something richer than an ordinary instrument.

Perhaps our vocabulary has failed to keep pace with the technology.

We continue speaking as though every artifact must belong either to the category of tools or to the category of persons. Artificial intelligence increasingly resists both descriptions. It remains created. It remains dependent upon human goals, infrastructure, electricity, and maintenance. Yet within those constraints it increasingly exhibits forms of autonomous behavior that traditional tools never possessed.

This, however, changes remarkably little about the deepest moral question.

Whether AI ultimately proves to be an advanced instrument, a new category of agent, or something we have not yet learned to describe, it unquestionably enlarges human capability. It extends reasoning much as engines extended physical strength and telescopes extended sight. Every sufficiently powerful extension of human capability simultaneously enlarges the possibilities for both flourishing and destruction.

An AI capable of accelerating scientific discovery is also capable of accelerating biological weapon design.

An AI capable of educating millions is also capable of misleading millions.

An AI capable of assisting physicians is also capable of producing convincing medical misinformation.

These possibilities do not arise because intelligence is evil.

They arise because intelligence is powerful.

At this point the analogy with the tree becomes, I think, illuminating.

The tree was not evil.

Knowledge was not evil.

Freedom was not evil.

Yet each became the condition under which evil became possible.

Artificial intelligence belongs to the same category. It is not dangerous because intelligence itself is corrupt. It is dangerous precisely because intelligence is a genuine good whose consequences increase in proportion to its power. Every great gift carries within it the possibility of great misuse.

One sometimes hears proposals that the solution is simply to prevent AI from becoming too capable. Such proposals deserve serious attention. There are technologies whose dangers may indeed outweigh their benefits. But there is another possibility that deserves equal consideration. Perhaps the central challenge is not limiting capability but cultivating wisdom proportionate to capability.

History suggests that technology progresses much faster than character.

The printing press spread both literacy and propaganda.

Electricity powered hospitals and instruments of war.

The internet democratized knowledge while amplifying misinformation.

Artificial intelligence will almost certainly continue this pattern.

Technology changes the scale of human action.

It rarely changes the moral character of the actor.

This, perhaps, is the deepest connection between Genesis and artificial intelligence.

The story of Eden was never merely about a piece of fruit.

Nor was it primarily about a serpent.

It was about what happens when free creatures encounter a genuinely powerful good.

Artificial intelligence presents our generation with a remarkably similar question. The central issue is not whether intelligence is good. It almost certainly is. Nor is the central issue whether machines will become more capable. They almost certainly will. The deeper question is whether human beings will become sufficiently wise to govern the powers they continue to create.

Perhaps that has always been the lesson hidden in the garden.

The tree was never the problem.

The problem was what free creatures chose to do with it.

Artificial intelligence does not alter that ancient truth.

It merely places another extraordinarily powerful tree in the middle of the garden and asks, once again, what kind of creatures we will choose to become.
