# What Object-Oriented Programming Taught Me

When I first learned C++, I did not understand why object-oriented programming existed. Procedural programming seemed entirely sufficient. If I wanted to solve another problem, I simply wrote another function. If I needed another feature, I added another method. The program grew as a sequence of actions, each function performing a specific task before passing control to the next. Classes, inheritance, and encapsulation seemed like unnecessary abstractions layered on top of something that already worked. It took me years to realize that object-oriented programming was not merely proposing another way to organize software. It was asking a fundamentally different question.

Procedural programming naturally asks, *What should happen next?* Object-oriented programming asks, *Who should be responsible for doing this?* At first, this appears to be a technical distinction. The more I reflected on it, the more it seemed to describe two very different ways of thinking about life itself. One organizes the world around actions. The other organizes it around identities.

Looking back, I sometimes feel that my own education has been overwhelmingly procedural. Every semester has taught me another method. Mathematics taught me how to reason abstractly. Robotics taught me how intelligent systems interact with the physical world. Embedded systems taught me how software meets hardware. Machine learning taught me how systems improve from data. Simulation taught me how to understand reality before building it. British literature taught me to pay attention to human character, motive, and tragedy. Each subject added another method, another function that I could call when needed.

For a long time, I thought that was the purpose of education: to accumulate more methods. Universities evaluate methods. Employers interview for methods. Résumés are little more than catalogs of methods. We ask whether someone knows C++, ROS, linear algebra, machine learning, or embedded programming. We rarely ask whether all these methods belong to a coherent person. Modern education is remarkably effective at teaching us how to do things, but much less interested in asking who we are becoming while we learn them.

This is precisely where object-oriented programming differs from procedural programming. In C++, a method cannot exist by itself. There is no floating `navigate()` somewhere in memory. There is no isolated `learn()`, `remember()`, or `design()`. Every method belongs to an object. The object gives the method its meaning. The same `move()` function means something entirely different when it belongs to a `Robot` than when it belongs to an `Aircraft`. Identity comes before action.

Perhaps human life works in much the same way. Kindness is not merely something one occasionally performs. It is characteristic of a kind person. Courage is not merely a successful response to danger. It belongs to someone who has become courageous. Curiosity is not simply reading another book or taking another class. It is a property of a curious mind. The methods matter because they express something deeper than themselves. They reveal the object to which they belong.

There have been moments when I wondered whether my interests were too scattered. Mathematics, robotics, embedded systems, machine learning, simulation, avionics, and literature appear, at first glance, to have very little in common. From the perspective of procedural programming, they are simply unrelated functions collected in the same file. But object-oriented programming suggests another possibility. Perhaps these disciplines are not disconnected methods at all. Perhaps they are different methods belonging to the same object. Mathematics develops precision. Robotics develops systems thinking. Embedded systems cultivate respect for physical constraints. Literature reminds us that every technology ultimately exists for human beings. The methods are different, but they may all be shaping the same person.

C++ also teaches that objects possess state. Every method changes the object on which it operates. Nothing begins from a blank slate. Every action leaves something behind that influences every action that follows. Human beings are remarkably similar. Every conversation changes us. Every failure modifies our confidence. Every success reshapes our ambitions. Every friendship alters the person who will make tomorrow's decisions. We often imagine that life is simply a sequence of independent achievements. In reality, each experience changes the object that experiences the next one.

Perhaps this is why wisdom differs from knowledge. Knowledge adds another method. Wisdom changes the object itself. A person may become increasingly capable while remaining fragmented. Another person may possess fewer methods but exhibit extraordinary coherence because everything they know belongs to the same underlying identity. Character, in this sense, is the architecture that gives unity to knowledge.

The longer I study computer science, the less I think object-oriented programming is merely about software engineering. It is about understanding that actions derive their meaning from identity. A well-designed C++ class is not admired because it possesses many methods. It is admired because those methods naturally belong together. Perhaps a well-lived life should be judged in much the same way. The goal is not simply to accumulate another skill, another project, or another achievement. The goal is to become the kind of person for whom those skills belong together naturally.

When I first learned C++, I thought object-oriented programming was about organizing code. I now suspect it was quietly asking a far more important question. Not *What should you do next?* but *Who are you becoming while you do it?* The methods eventually become obsolete. Technologies change. Programming languages evolve. But the object—the person those methods have gradually formed—remains.
