# Three Things on My Mind

## I. Resources Are Limited

Every civilization begins with the same condition.

Resources are finite.

There is only so much energy trapped beneath the earth, only so much sunlight falling upon it, only so much time before a star exhausts its fuel. Every species, every nation, every civilization competes—not necessarily against one another—but against the limits imposed by the universe itself.

Progress does not abolish these limits. It merely changes where they are encountered.

The industrial revolution did not eliminate scarcity; it converted coal into prosperity. The digital revolution did not free computation from physics; it transformed electricity into information. Artificial intelligence is simply the newest chapter in this story. Every generated token, every image synthesized, every agent reasoning for another minute consumes energy that cannot be spent elsewhere.

For a long time, these costs appeared negligible. Models were few, users were limited, and computation remained concentrated in research laboratories. But intelligence is becoming infrastructure. Billions of people will soon ask machines to reason on their behalf as routinely as they search the web today. At that scale, the unit of concern is no longer a single prompt. It is civilization.

This is, perhaps, one of the oldest lessons in history. Civilizations rarely disappear because they lack intelligence. They disappear because intelligence alone cannot overcome physical constraints. A society that continually consumes resources faster than it learns to use them wisely eventually discovers that nature is indifferent to ambition.

Artificial intelligence should not repeat that mistake.

This is why I became interested in [Joule](https://github.com/wuisabel-gif/Joule).

Joule begins from a simple conviction: energy should become a first-class metric of intelligence. We already measure latency, accuracy, and cost. Perhaps we should also ask a more fundamental question:

*Was this understanding worth the energy required to obtain it?*

The future of AI will not be determined solely by how intelligent our models become. It will also be determined by whether that intelligence can exist within the finite resources of the world that sustains it.

In the end, every sufficiently advanced civilization faces the same equation.

Not *How much intelligence can we create?*

But *How much intelligence can we sustain?*

## II. Memory and Communication Are Imperfect

No civilization survives by intelligence alone.

It survives because knowledge outlives the individuals who discovered it.

Every generation inherits books written by people it will never meet, scientific theories whose authors are long dead, laws established centuries earlier, and stories remembered across millennia. Human civilization is, in many ways, an external memory system. Without it, every generation would begin again.

Yet memory is never perfect.

Two people witness the same event and remember it differently. Nations preserve competing histories. Scientists revise accepted theories when new evidence appears. Memory is valuable not because it is infallible, but because it can be questioned, corrected, and refined.

An intelligent civilization must therefore solve a difficult problem.

*When memory disagrees with reality, which should change?*

If every memory is immutable, intelligence becomes dogma.

If every new observation erases the past, intelligence loses continuity.

Wisdom lies somewhere between the two: preserving experience while remaining willing to revise it when the world presents better evidence.

Artificial intelligence faces precisely the same dilemma.

An agent that remembers nothing repeats the same mistakes forever. An agent that remembers everything without questioning it eventually accumulates contradictions. Memory is not simply about storing information. It is about deciding what deserves to persist and what deserves to change.

This is why I built [MemWhale](https://github.com/wuisabel-gif/MemWhale).

MemWhale is not intended to be merely another database. It explores a more fundamental question:

*What does it mean for an artificial mind to possess a memory that is persistent, inspectable, revisable, and ultimately owned by the individual rather than by the model?*

But memory alone is insufficient.

Knowledge becomes civilization only through communication.

Human conversation is remarkably fluid. We interrupt. We clarify. We finish one another's sentences. We notice misunderstandings before they become disasters. We rarely wait patiently for another person to finish every thought before contributing our own.

Current AI systems do.

Once an agent begins reasoning, communication effectively stops. The human becomes an observer, waiting for the machine to finish before correcting its assumptions. Minutes of computation may be spent pursuing a misunderstanding that could have been resolved with a single sentence.

This is why I built [Delphin](https://github.com/wuisabel-gif/Delphin).

Delphin asks a deceptively simple question:

*Why should communication stop simply because reasoning has begun?*

Instead of treating conversation as alternating turns, Delphin treats it as a continuous process. Humans may continue speaking while the agent thinks. New information may wait, interrupt, or reshape the ongoing task before unnecessary work accumulates.

Civilization has never depended upon perfect memory or perfect communication.

It has depended upon systems that allow imperfect minds to remember together and to correct one another before error becomes history.

Perhaps artificial intelligence requires the same.

## III. Intelligence Alone Is Not Enough

For much of the history of computing, software was deterministic.

A program received an input, executed a sequence of instructions, and produced an output. Every decision had already been made by its programmer. The computer merely carried them out.

Artificial intelligence changes this relationship.

Increasingly, we are no longer writing programs that execute instructions. We are building systems that pursue objectives.

An agent is not simply software with a language model attached. It is software capable of making decisions under uncertainty. It observes, remembers, plans, acts, evaluates, and adapts. The programmer no longer specifies every step. Instead, the programmer designs the environment within which decisions are made.

This represents a profound shift.

For centuries, engineering has been the art of constructing machines.

Agentic engineering is becoming the art of constructing societies.

No civilization depends upon a single individual, no matter how intelligent. Cities exist because responsibilities are divided. Scientists rely upon engineers, engineers upon manufacturers, manufacturers upon logistics, and every profession upon countless others it will never meet. Complexity is managed not by making every person capable of everything, but by organizing intelligence into systems of cooperation.

Perhaps artificial intelligence will develop in much the same way.

The future may not belong to one infinitely intelligent model. It may belong to many specialized agents that communicate, remember, delegate, verify one another's work, and coordinate toward shared goals. Intelligence will emerge not only from reasoning, but from organization.

This is what draws me to agentic engineering.

The interesting questions are no longer simply:

*Can a model solve this problem?*

They become:

*How should agents divide work?*

*How should they communicate uncertainty?*

*When should they trust memory?*

*How should they verify one another?*

*How should they resolve disagreement?*

These are not merely software engineering questions. They are the same questions every civilization has had to answer throughout history.

Seen in this light, my projects no longer feel separate.

Joule asks how intelligence should exist within finite physical resources.

MemWhale asks how intelligence should remember.

Delphin asks how intelligence should communicate.

Agentic engineering asks the final question:

*How should intelligence organize itself?*

Perhaps Artificial General Intelligence will not arrive when a single model becomes sufficiently capable.

Perhaps it will emerge when memory, communication, coordination, and efficient use of finite resources become parts of a single coherent system.

If that is true, then the future of AI will resemble the history of civilization itself—not the story of one extraordinary mind, but of many minds learning how to think together.
