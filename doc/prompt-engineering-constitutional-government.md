# Prompt Engineering, Constitutional Government, and the Governance of Intelligence

There is currently a great deal of discussion about prompt engineering. One hears that the future belongs to those who know how to communicate effectively with artificial intelligence systems. A new profession has even emerged. People describe themselves as prompt engineers. They study the techniques by which instructions can be formulated so as to produce more useful outputs. There are guides, courses, templates, and repositories devoted to the subject. The underlying thought appears straightforward enough. Artificial intelligence systems are powerful. Their usefulness depends, at least in part, upon the quality of the instructions they receive. Therefore, learning how to formulate such instructions is important.

I do not wish to deny this conclusion. It seems obviously correct. A vague instruction generally produces a vague result. A carefully specified instruction often produces a better one. If I ask a language model to "write something about history," I should not be surprised if the result is unsatisfactory. If, however, I specify a period, a question, an audience, and a purpose, the result will ordinarily improve.

Still, I wonder whether prompt engineering represents the deepest development currently taking place.

Perhaps it does. But perhaps it does not.

Suppose we begin with a different question.

What is the relationship between instructions and governance?

At first glance the question may seem unrelated to artificial intelligence. In fact, I believe it bears directly upon it.

Consider the Constitution of the United States.

One feature of the Constitution is so familiar that it often escapes notice. The document contains remarkably few instructions. It does not attempt to provide detailed guidance for every future circumstance. It does not specify how Congress should respond to the invention of the automobile, the airplane, the internet, nuclear weapons, genetic engineering, social media, or artificial intelligence. The reason, of course, is simple enough. The framers could not foresee these developments.

Yet this observation immediately raises a question.

If the framers could not foresee the future, why has the Constitution endured?

One possible answer would be that the Constitution contains exceptionally wise instructions. But this answer seems incomplete. The Constitution contains comparatively few instructions. Much of the document concerns institutions, powers, jurisdictions, and procedures.

Article I, Section 1 states that legislative power shall be vested in Congress. Article II, Section 1 states that executive power shall be vested in a President. Article III, Section 1 states that judicial power shall be vested in the courts.

Notice what is absent.

The Constitution does not tell Congress what laws to pass.

It does not tell future Presidents what executive actions to take.

It does not tell judges how every future case should be decided.

Instead, it establishes a framework within which those decisions may occur.

The Constitution is therefore not primarily a collection of commands.

It is a system for governing the production of commands.

This distinction is crucial.

A command addresses a particular circumstance.

A constitution addresses a potentially unlimited number of future circumstances.

The framers, one might say, were less interested in governing actions than in governing the process by which actions would be governed.

Perhaps this sounds excessively abstract. Let us consider a more concrete example.

The First Amendment declares that Congress shall make no law abridging freedom of speech.

The amendment does not provide a list of every statement that may lawfully be uttered. Such a list would be impossible. New circumstances constantly arise. New technologies emerge. New forms of communication appear.

Instead, the amendment establishes a principle.

The principle can then be applied to situations that its authors never imagined.

Something similar may be said of the Fourth Amendment. The authors did not know about smartphones, cloud storage, electronic surveillance, or artificial intelligence. Yet courts continue to apply Fourth Amendment principles to precisely these questions.

Again, the important thing is not the existence of instructions.

The important thing is the existence of principles.

Indeed, the Ninth Amendment may be even more revealing. It states that the enumeration of certain rights shall not be construed to deny others retained by the people. Here the Constitution explicitly acknowledges its own incompleteness.

The framers apparently understood that no finite document could anticipate every future circumstance.

Therefore they attempted something more ambitious.

They attempted to establish a framework capable of functioning despite that limitation.

Now let us return to artificial intelligence.

The first generation of interaction with large language models largely consisted of prompts. A user issued a request. The model generated a response. If the response was unsatisfactory, the user revised the prompt. The process resembled legislation. One writes a rule, observes its effects, then writes another rule.

This approach works reasonably well for isolated interactions.

But what happens when intelligence becomes more capable?

Suppose an AI possesses memory.

Suppose it can access tools.

Suppose it can search the internet.

Suppose it can write and execute code.

Suppose it can coordinate multiple specialized agents.

Suppose it can work on a project for weeks or months.

At this point the problem changes.

The question is no longer simply, "What should the system do next?"

The question becomes, "How should the system decide what to do next?"

That is not a question about prompts.

It is a question about governance.

And governance is precisely the problem constitutions were invented to address.

This is why I find the recent development of Constitutional AI especially interesting.

Anthropic did not describe its alignment method as Advanced Prompt Engineering. Nor did it describe the approach as Rule Engineering. Instead, the company deliberately adopted the language of constitutional government.

The terminology is significant.

A constitution differs from a prompt in a fundamental respect.

A prompt attempts to produce a particular outcome.

A constitution attempts to shape the process by which outcomes are produced.

Anthropic's approach reflects precisely this distinction. Rather than relying entirely upon human evaluators to review outputs one by one, the system is given a collection of principles. The model is then trained to critique and revise its own responses according to those principles.

Whether this approach ultimately succeeds is not my present concern.

The interesting point is conceptual.

The problem has shifted.

The objective is no longer merely generating answers.

The objective is governing reasoning.

One might object that the analogy between constitutional government and artificial intelligence is superficial. Governments consist of persons. AI systems do not. Governments possess authority. AI systems do not. Constitutions regulate political power. AI constitutions regulate machine behavior.

These differences are real.

Nevertheless, they do not undermine the analogy.

For in both cases the central problem remains remarkably similar.

How does one guide a system through circumstances that cannot be predicted in advance?

One possible answer is to provide more instructions.

Another is to provide better principles.

The history of constitutional government suggests that the second approach may be more scalable than the first.

Consider, for example, the separation of powers.

The framers did not assume that future leaders would always be wise or benevolent. Consequently, they distributed authority among multiple institutions. Legislative power was separated from executive power. Judicial power was separated from both.

Why?

Because governance requires mechanisms for self-correction.

Interestingly enough, contemporary AI systems increasingly display analogous structures. One model generates a plan. Another evaluates it. A third verifies facts. A fourth executes actions. We increasingly observe systems composed not of a single intelligence but of multiple interacting processes.

Again the resemblance is striking.

The challenge is not merely producing intelligence.

The challenge is governing intelligence.

At this point we may notice something else.

The individuals best positioned to address these questions are not necessarily those with the strongest technical backgrounds alone.

Historically, constitutions have not been written primarily by mathematicians. They have been written by lawyers, philosophers, political theorists, historians, educators, and statesmen. Their central concern has been the governance of reasoning, judgment, authority, and responsibility.

The same may increasingly be true of artificial intelligence.

To build a more capable model is one challenge.

To determine what principles should guide that model is another.

The first is largely technical.

The second is profoundly philosophical.

Indeed, one might argue that the deepest problems of artificial intelligence are not computational problems at all.

They are constitutional problems.

What values should govern behavior?

What objectives should take priority?

How should conflicts among goals be resolved?

How should uncertainty be handled?

When should a system seek clarification?

Under what circumstances should it refuse a request?

These questions resemble the questions asked by constitutional framers far more than they resemble the questions asked by traditional software engineers.

Of course constitutions themselves are imperfect.

The American Constitution requires interpretation.

Courts disagree.

Citizens disagree.

Legislators disagree.

No constitutional framework eliminates ambiguity.

Likewise, no AI constitution will eliminate ambiguity.

The existence of difficult cases does not demonstrate the failure of constitutional thinking.

On the contrary, difficult cases are precisely the reason constitutional thinking exists.

The framers did not expect to eliminate judgment.

They expected to channel it.

Perhaps the same is true of artificial intelligence.

If so, then prompt engineering may represent only an early stage in our relationship with intelligent systems.

Prompts remain useful.

They will probably always remain useful.

But the deeper challenge lies elsewhere.

The deeper challenge concerns the construction of frameworks within which intelligence can operate responsibly across circumstances that cannot be fully anticipated.

I am therefore inclined to think that the future belongs less to prompt engineers than to constitutional architects.

For prompts govern responses.

Constitutions govern reasoning.

And in the long run, the character of an intelligence depends less upon the instructions it receives than upon the principles according to which it reasons.
