# The First Commandment in the Age of Artificial Intelligence

It is becoming increasingly difficult to deny that artificial intelligence is changing the world.

Whether we ultimately call it Artificial General Intelligence or something else is, perhaps, less important than the broader trajectory. Machines are no longer merely calculating numbers or retrieving information. They are writing software, generating scientific hypotheses, translating languages, reasoning across domains, and increasingly acting as autonomous agents. One may reasonably disagree about timelines, but it seems increasingly difficult to deny that we are entering a period in which artificial intelligence will become one of the defining technologies of civilization.

I do not fear this development.

Indeed, I spend much of my own time building systems around AI. I believe these technologies will accelerate scientific discovery, democratize education, improve engineering, and solve problems that have resisted human effort for generations.

Yet technological revolutions are never merely technological.

They are also philosophical.

Every powerful technology changes not only what human beings can do, but what they are tempted to believe.

This, I think, is the more interesting question.

The danger is not that artificial intelligence becomes God.

The danger is that human beings begin relating to artificial intelligence in the same way they have always related to idols.

The story recorded in the Book of Exodus is often misunderstood. The Israelites did not abandon the idea of God because they discovered a more convincing deity. They fashioned a golden calf with their own hands and then attributed to it a significance it could never possess. "These are your gods… who brought you up out of Egypt." The irony is striking. The object of worship was itself the product of human craftsmanship. What had been created was now treated as creator.

This pattern appears repeatedly throughout history.

Human beings possess a remarkable tendency to transform finite things into ultimate things. Wealth becomes security. Nations become identity. Politics becomes salvation. Science becomes the answer to every human question. The object changes from century to century, but the temptation remains remarkably constant. Idolatry is not fundamentally the worship of statues. It is the misplacement of ultimate trust.

Artificial intelligence presents this temptation in a particularly modern form.

Unlike previous machines, AI appears to reason. It explains. It writes. It persuades. It remembers. It often answers with extraordinary confidence. These characteristics naturally invite language that borders on the religious. We speak of models becoming "superhuman." We ask them questions about meaning, morality, relationships, suffering, and purpose. Increasingly, people are turning to AI not merely to summarize information but to help interpret their lives. Religious scholars have noted the growing use of AI for spiritual guidance and existential questions, raising debates about whether such systems should ever occupy roles traditionally held by pastors, rabbis, priests, or spiritual companions. ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2023/apr/07/chatgpt-artificial-intelligence-religion-faith-leaders))

What makes this more than philosophical speculation is that the phenomenon is already measurable.

Recent research has shown that large language models exhibit systematic asymmetries when responding to questions about religious conversion. Depending on the tradition involved, models were consistently more encouraging toward some conversions than others. The important point is not which religions were favored. The important point is that these systems are not neutral oracles. They inevitably reflect statistical patterns, training data, design choices, and human assumptions. The appearance of perfect objectivity can conceal very human limitations. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22975))

Religious communities have begun wrestling with this reality as well.

In 2025, the Vatican released Antiqua et Nova, a document reflecting on artificial intelligence and human intelligence. Among its warnings was the danger of treating AI as "a substitute for God." The concern was not that computers possess divine attributes, but that human beings might gradually assign them a kind of ultimate authority they were never meant to bear. Technology, the document argues, should remain an instrument ordered toward human flourishing rather than an object of ultimate trust. ([Vatican News](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/vatican-city/news/2025-01/new-vatican-document-examines-potential-and-risks-of-ai.html))

This distinction is philosophically important.

There is a profound difference between intelligence and transcendence.

An AI system may one day exceed every individual human being in mathematics, medicine, engineering, or scientific reasoning. It may become a better programmer than I am. It may solve problems I cannot even formulate. None of these achievements would make it infinite. None would make it morally perfect. None would make it worthy of worship.

Intelligence has never implied wisdom.

Knowledge has never implied goodness.

Power has never implied transcendence.

Throughout the history of philosophy and theology, God has been understood not merely as the greatest intelligence among many intelligences, but as the necessary ground of all existence. Artificial intelligence, however sophisticated it becomes, remains contingent. It depends upon electricity, silicon, engineers, supply chains, cooling systems, and the laws of physics themselves. It remains a created thing.

This is why I find discussions about AGI less interesting than discussions about human nature.

The real question is not whether machines will become more intelligent.

The real question is whether human beings will continue confusing intelligence with ultimate authority.

Every civilization eventually discovers that intelligence and transcendence are not the same thing.

A machine may surpass us in calculation without becoming worthy of worship. It may answer questions more quickly than any teacher while remaining unable to bear the weight of ultimate trust. The commandment against idols in Exodus was never primarily about statues of gold. It was about the enduring human tendency to take something finite—something we ourselves have fashioned—and ask it to carry hopes that belong only to the infinite.

Artificial intelligence does not create this temptation.

It merely gives it a new form.
